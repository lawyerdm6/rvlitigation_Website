Embezzlement Defense

Company Funds, Entrusted Assets, and Disputed Authority

Embezzlement Defense in California

Embezzlement allegations arise when someone is accused of fraudulently using property that was entrusted to them. Business owners, employees, partners, officers, and professionals can face questions about company payments, client funds, expense reimbursements, or trust accounts.

RV Litigation Group PC helps clients throughout California examine the financial records, understand the allegations and develop a defense. The analysis starts with ownership, authority, the relationship of trust, and the alleged intent. A financial shortfall alone does not explain who caused it or whether a criminal offense occurred.

What the Law Says

Entrusted Property and Fraudulent Use

Penal Code 503 defines the core offense. Related provisions address officers and agents (PC 504), people holding assets for another (PC 506), and employees (PC 508). The specific charging theory matters when evaluating access, authority, and alleged misuse.— Summary of California Penal Code 503–508

Claim of Right Has Limits

Penal Code 511 recognizes a good-faith claim of title when property was appropriated openly and avowedly, subject to the statute’s limits. It expressly does not excuse unlawful retention of another’s property to offset a demand. An unpaid commission is not automatic permission to take company money.— Summary of California Penal Code 511

Restoration and Punishment

An intention to return property does not by itself defeat an embezzlement allegation. Sections 512 and 513 address intended or actual restoration; qualifying early restoration may allow mitigation, not a defense. Section 514 generally ties punishment to theft rules and contains a special rule for public funds.— Summary of California Penal Code 512–514

Entrustment, Authority and Intent Shape the Defense

Embezzlement focuses on property that was entrusted to the accused and allegedly appropriated fraudulently. Access to an account, authority to make some payments and ownership of the funds are different questions. Counsel reviews the relationship under which the property was held and the limits on its permitted use.

An accounting discrepancy, disputed reimbursement or breakdown in controls may require investigation without proving fraudulent intent. But a claimed right to compensation does not necessarily authorize taking someone else’s property. The statutory limits on claim of right and the distinction between a defense and mitigation must be respected.

A repayment or proposed settlement also raises separate criminal and business issues. Counsel evaluates the amount, timing and purpose of repayment, the accuracy of the alleged loss and the effect of any agreement. A private complainant does not control the prosecutor’s decision.

Trace Property Without Assuming Every Transfer Is a Loss

A reliable reconstruction identifies the owner, each transfer, the recipient, any authorized business purpose and subsequent disposition. Gross transfers, personal benefit and claimed loss may be different figures. Copies of ledgers should be checked against bank records and underlying transactions rather than treated as self-explanatory.

Where business partners dispute authority, ownership or accounting claims may overlap with a criminal investigation. The defense should coordinate those matters without using demands in a related dispute or witness contact to interfere with the criminal process.

Distinguish Entrusted Access From Authorized Use

Entrustment concerns how the person obtained the property

Embezzlement differs from a simple taking because the property was entrusted to the accused through a qualifying relationship. Account access or job duties may explain possession without proving that every later transfer was fraudulent. Counsel identifies the assets, the scope of the entrusted role and the particular conversion alleged.

Informal company practices can create disputed authority

Expense approvals, owner distributions, advances and reimbursement practices may be documented inconsistently. Earlier permission and the person's understanding should be tested against contemporaneous communications and the company's actual rules. A belief that compensation was owed does not by itself authorize taking entrusted funds to pay it.

Temporary use and repayment need separate treatment

A temporary diversion can still present an embezzlement allegation. Returning money may affect financial issues or sentencing considerations without automatically undoing completed conduct. The defense therefore examines what was authorized and intended when the assets were used, rather than relying entirely on a later restoration.

Records That Help Us Evaluate the Matter

  • Account statements, transaction exports, invoices and original accounting files with available audit history.
  • Employment, agency, management or trust agreements describing custody and authority.
  • Approval messages, expense policies, reimbursement records and relevant access permissions.
  • The complainant’s calculation of loss, any independent reconciliation and records of repayments or property returned.

Start an inquiry with the notice or allegation, your role and any stated deadline. Preserve complete originals; arrange an appropriate transfer method before sending sensitive financial records.

Fictional California Examples

These fictional examples illustrate issues counsel may evaluate. They are not firm cases or results. County names identify the setting; they do not establish venue or a firm office.

Hypothetical: Alameda County: vendor payments are disputed

An Oakland bookkeeper is accused of redirecting vendor payments. Counsel traces account ownership and the payment chain, compares instructions with the ledger and examines whether the transfers were authorized and what the bookkeeper understood.

Hypothetical: Monterey County: property-management funds

A property manager in Monterey faces questions about money moved between accounts. Counsel reviews the management agreement, ownership of the funds, approvals and transaction purpose. A plan to replace the money is not treated as an automatic defense.

Morgan Hill, Santa Clara County — fictional example

A Morgan Hill manager transfers company funds to a personal account and describes them as an approved short-term advance. The owner denies approving it. Earlier advance practices, messages, payroll records and the transfer's timing could clarify entrustment, authority and alleged fraudulent use.
