Personal Guarantee & Loan Default Claims

Defense Against Personal Liability for a Guaranteed Business Debt

Signing a personal guarantee for a business loan, lease, or line of credit means your personal assets can be pursued even though the underlying debt belongs to a company. When the lender comes after you individually, the guarantee document itself — and how it was administered — determines your exposure.

What Is a Personal Guarantee?

A personal guarantee is a separate, individual promise to pay a debt if the primary borrower — typically a business entity — fails to do so. Lenders and landlords routinely require guarantees from business owners before extending credit or signing a commercial lease, precisely because it defeats the liability protection an LLC or corporation would otherwise provide.

RV Litigation Group PC defends individuals in San Jose, San Francisco, Palo Alto, Oakland, and throughout Santa Clara County and San Francisco County who have been sued personally on a guarantee after their business defaulted.

What the Law Says

Civil Code 2787 — Guarantee Defined

"A surety or guarantor is one who promises to answer for the debt, default, or miscarriage of another..." — California Civil Code Section 2787

California's suretyship statutes (Civil Code 2787-2856) govern personal guarantees and provide certain protections for guarantors — but most commercial guarantee agreements are drafted specifically to waive many of these statutory defenses. Whether a defense survives depends on the exact waiver language in the document you signed.

Payment vs. Collection Guarantees

A guarantee of payment allows the creditor to sue the guarantor immediately upon the borrower's default, without first pursuing the borrower. A guarantee of collection generally requires the creditor to exhaust remedies against the borrower first. Nearly all commercial lender and landlord guarantees are drafted as guarantees of payment, which significantly increases a guarantor's exposure and the speed at which a lawsuit can be filed.

Real-World Examples

Example 1 — Commercial Lease Guarantee in San Jose

A small business owner personally guaranteed her company's retail lease. When the business closed early, the landlord sued her individually for the remaining lease term. Counsel reviews the guarantee language and the landlord's duty to mitigate damages by re-letting the space, narrowing the amount actually owed.

Example 2 — Equipment Loan Default in Oakland

A business partner co-signed an equipment financing agreement for his company. After the business defaulted, the lender sold the repossessed equipment at a below-market price and sued him for the deficiency. Counsel challenges whether the disposition was conducted in a commercially reasonable manner.

Example 3 — Modified Loan Terms in San Francisco

A guarantor learns the lender materially modified the underlying loan — extending the term and increasing the interest rate — without his knowledge or consent after he signed the guarantee. Counsel evaluates whether this material alteration discharges or reduces his obligation under the guarantee.

Common defenses include improper notice of default, material alteration of the underlying loan terms without the guarantor's consent, release or impairment of collateral that increased the guarantor's risk, and, in some cases, fraud or duress in obtaining the guarantee.
